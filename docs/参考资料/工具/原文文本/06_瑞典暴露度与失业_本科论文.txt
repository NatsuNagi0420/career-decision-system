Generative AI exposure across occupational sectors and changes in unemployment in Sweden
Authors: Aron Resebo and Maryan Ali
HT2025 Bachelor thesis, 15 credits Subject: Economics √ñrebro University School of Business Supervisor: Daniela Andr√©n Grading teacher: Fredrik Andersson Examiner: Daniel Knutsson

Abstract
This thesis examines whether the development of generative artificial intelligence is associated with changes in average unemployment across occupational sectors in Sweden. Treating the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 as a common timing event, the study applies a two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences framework to monthly unemployment data across 22 occupational sectors. Occupational exposure to generative AI is measured using a task-based classification derived from existing literature, distinguishing between sectors with high and low exposure. The main empirical finding is that unemployment increased more on average in occupational sectors classified as highly exposed to generative AI relative to less exposed sectors following November 2022. The estimated difference-in-differences coefficient is statistically significant at the 5 percent level based on a two-sided t-test with standard errors clustered at the occupational-sector level but should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of clusters and potential heterogeneity across sectors. The findings contribute with post-2022 information from the Swedish labor market to the literature on artificial intelligence and unemployment, suggesting that generative AI may be associated with short-term labor market disruptions, even if long-run productivity gains and task reallocation may offset these impacts.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence have raised concerns about their potential effects on labor market outcomes, particularly unemployment across occupations with differing exposure to automation technologies. While a growing empirical literature examines the relationship between technological change and employment, most existing studies rely on data ending before the public release of large-scale generative AI systems such as ChatGPT in late 2022. As a result, there is limited empirical evidence on whether the recent big number of generative AI model launches is associated with observable changes in unemployment patterns across occupations in the period following its widespread availability.
1.1 Purpose and research question
The purpose of this paper is to examine whether unemployment evolved differently across occupational sectors with varying exposure to generative artificial intelligence following the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 (Lodefalk, 2024). Using panel data on occupational sectors from Sweden between January 2021 through October 2025, the analysis investigates whether occupational sectors classified as highly exposed to generative AI experienced changes in unemployment relative to less exposed occupations in the postNovember 2022 period. While previous studies on automation, employment and unemployment rely primarily on data ending in or before 2022 (Alekseeva et al., 2021; Felten et al., 2021; Acemoglu et al., 2022; Georgieff and Hyee, 2022; Engberg et al., 2025), this study extends the existing methodology by incorporating more recent data, trying to find indications on unemployment in occupational sectors associated with exposure to generative AI technologies.
1.2 Empirical strategy
The empirical strategy is aimed at identifying average changes in unemployment between highly and weakly AI-exposed occupational sectors, using the release of ChatGPT in November 2022 as the common treatment timing. Occupational sectors are classified as either highly or weakly exposed to generative AI based on task-based measures from the existing
5

literature later described. The treatment group consists of highly exposed occupations, while less exposed occupations serve as the control group. Setting up the difference-in-differences estimator this way measures the average change in unemployment between highly and weakly AI-exposed occupational sectors after November 2022 (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Wooldridge, 2019; Cunningham, 2021).
1.3 Limitations
Job vacancies from Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen are used as a proxy for labor demand, but not all vacancies are posted through this channel. It is previously estimated that only 30-50% of all vacancies are posted via Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen (Tillv√§xtverket 2021). The AI exposure measure is based on task-based indices originally developed for U.S. occupations and adapted to Swedish SSYK codes. This mapping may involve measurement error, and the binary classification of high versus low exposure simplifies what may be a continuous and heterogeneous degree of AI exposure. The analysis treats the release of ChatGPT in November 2022 as an exogenous shock, when it is possible that adoption of generative AI is gradual and may differ across firms and occupations (OECD 2025). The study is aimed at capturing occupational sector-level short-run effects and is unlikely to identify individuallevel displacement, task reallocation within occupations, or long-run structural changes in unemployment. The empirical specification includes a time-varying control to capture shortrun occupational dynamics. While this may improve precision, it changes the estimand relative to a baseline difference-in-differences design, implying that the estimated effects should be interpreted as conditional associations rather than unconditional associated effects.
1.4 Assumptions
This study relies on several identifying assumptions necessary for the difference-indifferences design to estimate credible average effect of generative AI exposure on unemployment in different occupational sectors. Most importantly, in the absence of generative AI, unemployment in highly and weakly AI-exposed occupations is assumed to have evolved in parallel over time, labelled the parallel trends assumption (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Wooldridge, 2019; Cunningham, 2021). It is also assumed that highly and weakly AI-
6

exposed occupational sectors did not systematically adjust employment patterns in anticipation of the release prior to November 2022. Spillovers between occupational sectors are assumed to be limited, such that the exposure in one occupational sectors does not directly affect unemployment in other occupational sectors. Exposure is assumed to be uncorrelated with post-treatment shocks to unemployment, conditional on the included fixed effects. Unobserved effects are also assumed to affect all occupational sectors in the same way (Angrist & Pischke, 2009).
1.5 Relevance
Since most of the recent studies focus on data up to or prior to 2022 (Alekseeva et al. 2021; Acemoglu et al. 2022 & Lodefalk et al. 2024), there are good reasons to try and keep research on the topic of AI‚Äôs effects on the labor market up to date (Lodefalk et al. 2024). Findings from Hoi, Reshel and Zhou (2024) indicate that a broader labor displacement may occur in targeted sectors, that is, sectors with high exposure to AI as defined by previous studies, when the analysis is using up-to-date data.

2. Theoretical background
2.1 A short introduction to AI
A short definition of AI is in place in order to better understand the technology and its potential impact on labor, in terms of demand. Artificial intelligence, AI, is a computerized system that solves problems that traditionally demand human intelligence. AI uses large amounts of data in order to make predictions and can thereby give recommendations or even make decisions based on its results (Lodefalk, 2024). The term generative AI refers to specific AI models that are capable of generating content such as text, images, video or audio from data (Feuerriegel et al. 2024; Brynjolfsson et al. 2023). Examples of generative AI models include DALL-E 2, GPT-4, Grok and LLaMA (Wang, 2024; Ghaseminejad, 2025). An important class of generative AI is large language models, LLMs. An LLM is trained to predict the next word in a sequence, given what has come before, using a big database of text (Lodefalk, 2024). This enables it to generate grammatically correct and meaningful text. While the term emphasizes human language, the same methods can apply to other sequential data (Brynjolfsson et al. 2023).
2.2 The effect of AI on labor
There have been two important sides to what impact AI will have on labor in terms of supply and demand. On one side, Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019) argues that development of AI up until 2019 was done towards automation, with decreasing labor demand as a consequence. On the other side, Lodefalk (2024) suggests that while AI may displace some existing jobs and duties, it is also likely to generate other forms of jobs. Brynjolfsson & Mitchell (2017) means, in line with Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), that whenever a task is possible to be done by some version of machine learning, and it is cheaper to do so, profit-maximizing entrepreneurs and managers will seek to substitute labor with machines. A more dramatic phrasing about the situation is done by Mutascu and Hegerty (2023) where they express that even professions that people ought to be ‚Äúsafe‚Äù, such as higher-level thinking and organizational work might be at risk if AI becomes sufficiently advanced, underlining the importance to better seek understanding of its potential impact on the labor market. Taken together, these perspectives
8

illustrate that AI‚Äôs impact on job vacancies and unemployment reflects a complex situation where AI might both displace and create jobs, not simply reducing the demand for labor.
2.3 AI and productivity
Building on the information on what potential impact AI might have on labor in section 2.2, medium-term productivity gains from AI are expected to be positive but modest in aggregate and heterogeneous across economies (Misch et al., 2025). The short-term effects from productivity increase on a micro level, i.e. specific sectors or occupations, suggest that it might be upwards of as much as 50% in the case of software engineers (Misch et al., 2025). A study by Gao and Feng (2023) finds that for every 1% increase in AI penetration across manufacturing industries in China is associated with a total factor productivity increase of about 14.2%. In labor-market terms, productivity improvements tend to increase labor demand via lower unit costs and higher output, while automation reduces demand for specific tasks and occupations (Pissarides, 2000; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019). Depending on the occupational sector and task in which AI is able to improve productivity, the direct net effect might be ambiguous in terms of vacancies and unemployment (Agrawal et al., 2019).
2.4 Timeline of recent developments
There has been a drastic increase in launches and available AI models between 2022 and 2024 (Ghaseminejad Raeini, 2025). A sample of these models is listed in table 1. Considering that many of these models were released from 2022- onwards, their presumed impact on unemployment or labor demand (Feuerriegel et al. 2024) remains difficult to empirically assess due to their recent deployment and limited data. Worth mentioning in addition to this table is that after the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022, OpenAI had within two months over 100 million subscribers (Engberg et al. 2025), adding some perspective to the possible adoption-rate from that moment forward.

2.5 Conclusion of theoretical background
The theoretical background helps defining artificial intelligence as a technology capable of performing non-routine tasks that traditionally require human intelligence through data-driven decision-making (Lodefalk, 2024). Special attention has been given to generative AI and large language models which are capable of generating output in form of text, images, audio and video (Brynjolfsson et al. 2023; Feuerriegel et al. 2024). The recent developments of AImodels demonstrate a rapid expansion in terms of availability (Ghaseminejad Raeini, 2025; Wang, 2024). The different sides of arguments to what impact this will have on vacancies and unemployment, caused by either automation or productivity gains, together with the important timeline of developments composed from Ghaseminejad Raeini (2025) and Wang (2024) suggests that there is still limited empirical analysis displaying uniform conclusions. This further highlights the importance to seek better understanding of this development and its potential impact on vacancies and unemployment.

3. Previous studies
3.1 Demand for AI skills in the labor market
Alekseeva et al. (2021) studied the demand for AI skills in USAs labor market using data from job ads and if there is a posted wage premia in occupations that require at least some set of AI skills. Their definition of AI-related skills comes from key words in job ads such as: Artificial Intelligence, machine vision, deep learning, image processing among others. The study was conducted using data on posted job ads from Burning Glass Technologies, now Lighcast, between January 2010 to July 2019. The key findings from this study were that they saw an increasing trend in the share of posted job ads that required AI skills over time, from less than 0.1 % in 2010 to about 0.6% in 2019. This study also gives a visual representation of how the AI required skill share evolve by different industry sectors and occupations over time, highlighting that sectors like information, finance and insurance are among those who seek most AI skills. Overall, these findings point towards AI as a complementary technology; firms seek to value and demand labor who can work with AI skills, in line with the idea presented by Lodefalk (2024), rather than that AI would work as a replacement for labor.
3.2 Measuring occupational exposure to artificial intelligence
Felten et al. (2021) approaches the potential effects of AI on labor markets by developing a new measure called AIOE, AI Occupational Exposure, which links common AI applications to workplace abilities across occupations. This measure indicates the likelihood that an occupation interacts with AI, without judging whether AI complements or substitutes human labor. The measure is constructed by linking widely used AI applications, identified through the Electronic Frontier Foundation, EFF, AI Progress Measurement project, which tracks and categorizes the development of AI technologies, to data on occupational abilities from the Occupational Information Network, O*NET, a U.S. Department of Labor database that provides detailed information on the skills, tasks, and abilities required for different occupations in the modern-day American workplace. Key findings include that the occupations with highest exposure to AI include genetic counselors, financial examiners and actuaries. The occupations with lowest exposure to AI were dancers, fitness trainers and
11

aerobics instructors. On an aggregate level, they constructed a similar AI exposure measure called AIIE, AI Industry Exposure, where the results suggested that securities and other financial institutions had the highest exposure as opposed to crop production which had the lowest exposure. As a concluding remark they emphasize that there is still little systematic evidence of what impact AI and automation will have on the labor force, but that the AIOE and AIIE measures can be a useful tool to explore the circumstances in which AI may or may not substitute or complement human labor.
3.3 AI adoption and employment effects in online vacancies.
Acemoglu et al. (2022) studies the adoption of AI in USA and its effects on the labor market. They also use data from Lightcast but with a bit shorter time span, from 2010 through 2018. They approach AI adoption by linking a task-based perspective to a task structure of an establishment. Key findings include that AI-exposed establishments simultaneously reduce hiring in non-AI positions. This indicates that AI adoption does displace some tasks internally. At an aggregate level however, the study detects no sizable impact on employment or wages across industries or occupations, suggesting that the scale of AI adoption in this period is still too small to reshape the labor market as a whole.
3.4 Short-run employment effects of generative AI
Hoi, Reshel and Zhou (2024) investigate if there is empirical evidence on the short-term effects of generative AI on the labor market using data on job ads scraped from the site Upwork, which posts freelance projects available and corresponding payment per project. They found that since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022, freelancers working in writing-related services experienced a decrease of 2% in the number of monthly jobs posted and a decrease of 5.2% in monthly earnings, compared to other freelancers in different occupations, both with statistical significance. They make a similar approach to the generative purpose models DALL-E 2, which can create realistic original images and art from a text description. It can combine concepts, attributes and styles (OpenAI 2025), and Midjourney, which works in a similar way as DALL-E 2 but is developed by the company Midjourney Inc.
12

(Midjourney 2025). These findings suggest that there might be some adverse effects in terms of employment with the rapid advance and adoption of this new technology.
3.5 Evidence on AI, hiring, and employment in Sweden.
Lodefalk et al. (2024) investigate how artificial intelligence influences hiring patterns in Sweden using job-posting data from the Swedish Public Employment Service (AF) covering 2014‚Äì2022. They find that establishments with greater exposure to AI increase hiring in both AI-related and non-AI roles, without any reduction in overall employment. This contrasts with Acemoglu et al. (2022), who show that in the United States, higher AI exposure is associated with lower non-AI hiring, suggesting labor displacement. Taken together, the evidence implies that in Sweden, AI appears to complement labor rather than substitute it.
3.6 Summary of previous studies.
The reviewed literature highlights that AI‚Äôs impact on labor markets varies, but the Swedish study stands out. While studies from the United States and internationally document rising demand for AI skills and signs of task-level displacement, such as reduced non-AI hiring (Acemoglu et al., 2022) or short-term labor displacement in freelance markets (Hoi et al. 2024), Sweden appears to follow a different pattern. Lodefalk et al. (2024) finds that Swedish establishments with higher AI exposure increase hiring in both AI and non-AI sectors without reducing overall employment. In contrast to U.S. findings, this suggests that in Sweden AI functions primarily as a complementary technology, supporting labor rather than replacing it. However, most data used in these previous studies, except for the one done by Hoi et al. (2024), are prior to or up until 2022, where much of the generative AI was launched to a broader public and potentially having a much bigger impact onwards.

4. Data
4.1 Dataset and sources
This study uses monthly administrative data from the Swedish Public Employment Service (Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen). Both datasets span 58 months (January 2021 to October 2025), providing 22 months of pre- treatment data (before ChatGPT‚Äôs release in November 2022) and 36 months of post-treatment data. Unemployment data are sourced from The Swedish Public Employment. Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen, measuring the total number of registered unemployed individuals by occupational category. Job vacancies data are also obtained from Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen and capture all advertised vacancies registered on their platform. Both datasets are disaggregated by occupational category (yrkesomr√•den) following the Swedish Standard Classification of Occupations (SSYK 2012). The data are publicly available and accessed through Arbetsf√∂rmedlingens statistical database in November 2025.
4.2 Sample construction
The final dataset is constructed by merging unemployment and vacancy data at the occupation-month level, resulting in a balanced panel of 1,276 observations. The sample consists of 22 occupational sectors based on the aggregated SSYK-groups used by Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen. (See Table 2: AI exposure Classification).
4.3 Outcome variables and control variables
Outcome and control variables: Unemployment (unemployed) is the primary outcome variable and is measured as the monthly number of registered unemployed individuals per occupational sector. Vacancies measure the monthly number of advertised job vacancies
14

per occupational sector. To account for skewness and facilitate interpretation, log transformed versions of both variables are also constructed and used in supplementary specifications. Key explanatory variables: AI exposure is defined as a binary indictor equal to 1 for occupations classified as highly exposed to generative AI, and 0 otherwise (1= high exposure, 0 = low exposure. The classification is based on occupation task characteristics assigned to occupational categories following prior research by Alekseeva et al. (2021); Felten et al. (2021); Acemoglu et al. (2022); Georgieff & Hyee (2022) and Engberg et al. (2025). Seven occupational groups are classified as highly exposed. These are predominantly cognitive, text-intensive, or analytical tasks where generative AI can feasibly substitute or complement human labor. The remaining 14 occupational groups are classified as low exposure (AI_exp = 0), reflecting a greater reliance as their core tasks involve physical, manual, or interpersonal activities with limited generative AI applicability. (See Table 2: AI exposure Classification) Post period indicator (Post) is a binary indicator equal to 1 for all months from November 2022 onward, representing the period after ChatGPT's public release. The interaction term AI_Post ( AI exposure x post) constitutes the treatment variable in the difference-indifference framework and captures differential changes in labour-market outcomes for highly exposed occupations after the introduction of generative AI. The Lagged controls: To account for occupation-specific labour-market dynamics, the model includes lagged values of the outcome variable. Specifically,lagged vacancies are included when unemployment is the dependent variable. These lagged controls are predetermined with respect to current treatment status and capture short-run market-labour momentum and occupation-specific trends.

4.5 Assessment of data reliability and limitations
Limitations of the dataset concern potential measurement error in the classification of AI exposure across occupational groups. In this analysis aggregated SSYK occupational categories have been used to construct the binary AI exposure indicator by mapping taskbased exposure measures from previous studies by Alekseeva et al. (2021); Felten et al. (2021); Acemoglu et al. (2022); Georgieff & Hyee (2022) and Engberg et al. (2025). Each occupational category may contain substantial heterogeneity in tasks and job roles with varying degrees of AI relevance. As a result some occupations may be misclassified as either high or low AI exposure. This may likely bias estimated treatment effects, which may lead to an underestimation of the true treatment effect. In addition, spillover effects across occupations cannot be ruled out. The adoption of generative AI in highly exposed occupations may indirectly affect labor demand and unemployment in less exposed but related occupations through relocation of tasks. Such cross occupation spillovers would violate the stable unit of treatment value assumption underlying the difference-in-differences framework and would tend to bias the estimated treatment effects toward zero. Therefore, the estimated difference-in-differences coefficients should be interpreted as conservative estimates of the impact generative AI has on unemployment.
4.6 Institutional definition, selection, and occupational measurement
The unemployment data used in this study come from Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen‚Äôs register-based verksamhetsstatistik, administrative activity statistics, and do not constitute official labormarket statistics in the sense of Statistics Sweden (SCB). Sweden‚Äôs official unemployment rate is produced by SCB through the Labour Force Survey, AKU, whereas Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen‚Äôs statistics consist of all individuals who are registered in the agency‚Äôs database as unemployed (Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen, 2026). Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen‚Äôs statistics count all individuals registered as unemployed on the last day of the month, regardless of concurrent employment (Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen, 2026). Formally, Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen defines inskrivna arbetsl√∂sa as the sum of openly unemployed individuals and those who are unemployed while participating in active labor-market programs, arbetsl√∂sa i program (Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen, 2026). This introduces a selection mechanism that is relevant for identification. Registration is voluntary and linked to eligibility for benefits and program participation, and different
18

occupational sectors may differ in their tendency to register when unemployed. In particular, workers in highly AI-exposed occupations, such as IT, managers and engineers, may be more likely to rely on private recruitment channels and may therefore be less likely to register than workers in more routine-based occupations (Tillv√§xtverket, 2021). If generative AI imposes changes in job search behaviour across occupations after 2022, part of the measured postNovember effect may operate through changes in registration behaviour rather than through job loss alone. Another institutional issue concerns how unemployment is assigned to occupational sectors. In Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen‚Äôs registers, individuals are classified by their registered occupational background, yrkesbakgrund, which typically reflects the most recently stated or historically recorded occupation, not necessarily the occupation the individual is currently seeking (Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen, 2026). Occupational codes are updated only when jobseekers revise the information, and updates may occur with delay. During periods of structural change, such as the broader launch of generative AI models, displaced workers may actively seek jobs outside their previous occupation while remaining coded under their old one. This would create measurement error in the Ì†µÌ∞¥Ì†µÌ∞ºÌ†µÌ±íÌ†µÌ±•Ì†µÌ±ùÌ†µÌ±ñ-variable so that the estimated difference-in-differences coefficient captures changes in registered unemployment by registered occupational background, not necessarily changes in true unemployment by current task or job. Because both registration and occupational updating may respond ambiguously to technological change, the direction of bias is theoretically unknown. Nonetheless, since Arbetsf√∂rmedlingen applies the same definitions and coding rules consistently over time and across occupations, the difference-in-differences framework remains informative about relative changes across occupational sectors after November 2022.
5. Method
To estimate the effect of generative AI on unemployment outcomes across occupational sectors, this study uses a difference-in-differences framework with two-way fixed effects. The identification strategy follows recent empirical work on AI and labour markets, which exploits sudden technological shocks to measure heterogenous occupational-sector exposure. We focus on the public release of ChatGPT on November 2022. This serves as a hypothetical and exogenous shock that rapidly and unexpectedly increased awareness and adoption of advanced generative AI tools among firms and workers. By using this sharp timing, the
19

difference-in-differences design compares changes in mean unemployment outcomes between occupational sectors classified as highly exposed and low exposed, after November 2022.
5.1 Identification and estimation strategy
The analysis is based on a two-way fixed effects difference-in-differences framework comparing highly AI-exposed occupations with less exposed ones before and after November 2022, when generative AI became publicly available. Occupation fixed effects absorb all time-invariant differences across occupational groups, such as long run differences in skill. Month fixed effects control for macroeconomic shocks that affect all occupations simultaneously. The model is estimated using ordinary least squares, OLS. Standard errors are clustered at the occupation level to address serial correlation and within occupational sector dependence over time. To improve the credibility of the estimates in the setting with a binary, time-varying treatment, the model includes lagged values of the outcome variable. These lagged controls help capture occupation-specific labor market momentum and reduce serial correlation, while remaining predetermined with regards to current treatment. This modeling choice aligns with recent DiD research on time varying treatments (Cerulli &Ventura, 2019) and is consistent with empirical implementations in Hui, Reshef & Zhou (2024). Identification of the treatment effects relies on the parallel-trends assumption, meaning that in the absence of the AI shock, labor-market outcomes in highly exposed and less exposed occupations would have evolved similarly over time. In the data some observations are classified as having ‚Äúundefined‚Äù occupations. In the baseline specification, these observations are retained in the sample and treated as a separate occupational category to preserve the full panel structure and avoid losing sectors that are relevant for inference. However, because this category groups together heterogenous workers, we also estimate all models excluding observations with ‚Äúundefined‚Äù occupation as a robustness check. These results are reported in the appendix, table 9, and are used to assess whether the main findings are sensitive to how these observations are handled.

Conceptually, selection bias is particularly relevant in this setting because AI-exposed sectors are likely inherently different from non-AI-exposed sectors in ways that may affect unemployment independently of generative AI. These sectors may for example be more likely to adopt digital technologies, accelerate automation, and respond more rapidly to structural shifts in post-COVID digitalization trends. If such differences in underlying dynamics are not fully accounted for, the observed post-November 2022 differences in average unemployment could reflect pre-existing sectoral trends rather than the causal effect of generative AI. The difference-in-differences design may mitigate this concern by comparing changes over time within sectors, but the validity of the estimates still hinges on the parallel trends assumption (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Cunningham, 2021), that in the absence of generative AI, AI-exposed and non-AI-exposed sectors would have experienced similar unemployment trends.
8.4 Weighting
The baseline difference-in-differences specification in section 5.2 is estimated by ordinary least squares, assigning equal weight to each sector‚Äìtime observation. Consequently, the estimated Ì†µÃÇÌªø-coefficient identifies the average treatment effect across occupational sectors rather than across the individuals represented in those sectors, as differences in sector size are not accounted for in the estimation. Larger and smaller sectors therefore contribute equally to the identifying variation. This approach may yield a valid average sector estimand, but it does not account for heterogeneity in sector size, which may be relevant for how the average individual in each sector is affected. Angrist and Pischke (2014) and Stock and Watson (2020) emphasize that the weighted least squares estimator is more efficient than ordinary least squares only under restrictive conditions, most importantly when the conditional variance of the error term is known or correctly specified (Stock & Watson, 2020). In practice, this requires to correctly model the heteroskedastic structure of the error term and use weights proportional to the inverse of the conditional error variance (Stock & Watson, 2020). In difference-in-differences settings however, treatment occurs at group level and outcomes are observed repeatedly over time, making the error structure both heteroskedastic and serially correlated in ways that are difficult to model. Also, the introduction of generative AI in November 2022 may itself alter the variance of outcomes across groups and over time. Under these conditions, weighting by outcome levels in unemployment are unlikely to correspond to
31

the true conditional variance function. As a result, weighted least squares does not generally deliver efficiency gains and may perform worse than unweighted ordinary least squares (Angrist & Pischke, 2014), but for the purpose of at least considering the difference, the regression output from using weighted least squares is shown in table 8, in the appendix. Interpreting the results from this table implies that larger occupational sectors experienced smaller average post-November 2022 differences in unemployment compared to smaller occupational sectors, holding everything else constant. Weighting the regression by sector size can be interpreted as approximating the average effect across workers rather than across sectors (Angrist & Pischke, 2014). Keeping inference at sector level, the model specification compares changes at group level between those that are more AI-exposed compared to those that are non-AI-exposed after November 2022. . 9. Conclusion
This study set out to examine whether the rapid developments in generative artificial intelligence, started off by the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022, is associated with changes in unemployment outcomes across occupational sectors in Sweden. The main empirical finding is that unemployment increased more on average in occupational sectors that were highly exposed to generative AI relative to less exposed after November 2022, but the results ought to be interpreted with caution. The empirical design implies a sharp posttreatment indicator that treats the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 as a technological shock when actual adoption is plausible to be gradual and heterogeneous across occupational sectors (Agrawal et al., 2019), implying that the estimates may capture short term adjustments in unemployment across sectors rather than the causal effect of adoption itself. Exposure to generative AI across occupational sectors is measured using a binary taskbased indicator that classifies occupations as either highly or weakly exposed based on previous literature and does not account for within-occupational sector differences and might thus overstate the average effect of generative AI on unemployment as it is empirically designed. Adding weights to each occupational sector indicates that the average effect from generative AI on unemployment is lowered and renders the difference-in-differences estimator insignificant but given the structure of the data, Stock & Watson (2020) and Angrist & Pischke (2009) warns about its unreliability. Inference is made using 22 occupational sectors
32

as the clustering unit. The limited number of clusters may also affect the reliability of the cluster-robust inference (Angrist & Pischke, 2009, 2014), despite taking cautious steps to avoid over stating statistical significance. The empirical framework all together relies heavily on the parallel trends assumption, which cannot be stressed enough according to Angrist & Pischke (2009, 2014) and Cunningham (2021), and cannot be tested entirely since the counterfactual outcome of what would have happened to the AI exposed occupational sectors, had they not been exposed to generative AI is ambiguous. All that being said, this study with this set of definitions on AI exposure across these 22 occupational sectors using previously stated controls, we find that occupational sectors that are more exposed to generative AI were associated with a higher average unemployment relative to those that are less exposed to generative AI after November 2022, with statistical significance at a 5% level using a twosided hypothesis test with 21 degrees of freedom. This does not imply that generative AI unanimously affects unemployment in these sectors altogether, but it highlights that the transition may be uneven and disruptive, reinforcing the need for continuous empirical monitoring and policy frameworks that support knowledge of rapid technological change, as is mentioned in almost all previous research in this study. These findings contribute to the literature on artificial intelligence and labor markets by providing post-2022 insights from the Swedish labor market. Prior studies using pre-2022 data generally find limited or no aggregated negative unemployment effects of AI adoption, both internationally (Acemoglu et al., 2022; Felten et al., 2021) and in Sweden specifically (Lodefalk et al., 2024). In contrast, recent evidence from Hui, Reshef & Zhou (2024) suggests that generative AI may have immediate adverse effects on unemployment and earnings in exposed tasks. The results of this study are suggestive of the latter findings, that generative AI may generate short-run labormarket disruptions that were not fully captured in earlier analyses of narrower or less capable AI technologies. In the long run, productivity gains and task reallocation may offset or reverse these effects, a possibility also emphasized by Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb (2019) and Lodefalk (2024). These findings could also point to several directions for future research. An important direction is to potentially move beyond sector-level data and study individual workers or firms. This would help to better understand whether the observed effects reflect actual job losses or rather changes in task composition within existing jobs. Future work could also use more direct measures of AI adoption, such as firms-level usage of generative AI tools instead of relying on exposure proxies based on task characterizes. Finally, as generative AI adoption becomes more widespread, longer observation periods should be studied in order to fully assess whether the short-run labor market disruptions documented here are followed by
33
